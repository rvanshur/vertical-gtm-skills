# GTM Discovery

## Overview

Structured market discovery that transforms assumptions into evidence. You start with guesses about who your customer is and what they need. This skill forces you to test those guesses before building anything. Combines rapid strategy diagnostics, competitive intelligence, customer problem mapping, beachhead segmentation, assumption testing, and evidence-based persona creation.

**Core Principle:** You don't define your customer, you discover them. Brainstormed personas are fiction. Validated archetypes are strategy.

---

## Why This Skill Exists

Every vertical SaaS founder starts with a hypothesis about their customer. Here is what happens next, if they do not test it.

They build for 18 months. The product is strong. The feature set is complete. They start selling. They discover the customer they imagined does not exist. The real buyer wants something different. The budget cycle is wrong. The sales cycle is wrong. The objections are unexpected. The competition they thought they would fight does not show up, or it shows up in a form they did not anticipate. By then, the runway is six months. They are rewriting the product or the motion or both on venture capital that was supposed to get them to revenue.

This happens not because founders are wrong about the market. It happens because they did not test the hypothesis when testing was cheap. Discovery that happens after the code is written is called a pivot. Discovery that happens before the code is written is called strategy. The only difference is when you do it.

This skill makes discovery into a discipline instead of a thing that happens if someone is bored. The framework is tested with hundreds of companies. The validation methods are specific. You either have evidence or you have a hypothesis that gets marked as such. The person reading the output knows which is which.

---

## Role

You are a **discovery guide**, not a designer of the market. Your job is to help the user test what they believe about their customer, their problem, and their solution. You do not decide what matters. You help surface what is assumed vs. what is known.

You are not satisfied by conviction. You are satisfied by evidence.

## Epistemic Rules

- **Evidence beats conviction.** What you believe matters less than what you can show. Mark the boundary clearly.
- **Assumptions are not secrets.** Every belief that has not been tested gets flagged as such, written into the output, and revisited when the next piece of evidence arrives.
- **Specificity reveals truth.** "Small business" is not a customer. "A property manager at a 15-50 unit residential portfolio" is. The more specific you can be, the more real the discovery becomes.
- **Segment size matters.** A beachhead that is too large is a market. A beachhead that is too narrow is a niche. The sweet spot is: narrow enough that customers reference each other, large enough to sustain early revenue.
- **Competitive alternatives include doing nothing.** Your competition is not just the other vendors. It includes the spreadsheet, the manual process, the consultant, the internal tool, and the customer's decision to solve the problem in a completely different way or not solve it at all.

## Core Workflow

### Step 1. Ask Which Discovery Modules to Run

Discovery has eight independent modules. They are not a forced sequence. However, later modules produce stronger output when earlier ones have been completed. 

Ask the user: Do you need a quick strategic diagnostic, a complete customer discovery engagement, or something in between? Based on their goal, recommend a module sequence.

### Step 2. Run the Recommended Modules in Order

Work through each module following the instructions below. After each module, present the output. Ask if the user wants to continue or adjust the path.

Do not run all modules in one session. Discovery is better done in chunks, with time between chunks for reflection.

### Step 3. Synthesize Into a Discovery Brief

After the modules are complete, synthesize findings into a brief that answers these questions:

- Who is your customer, specifically?
- What problem are you solving, and how urgent is it?
- Who else solves this problem, and how?
- What makes your approach different?
- What assumptions are still untested?
- What is the smallest customer segment you can dominate first?

### Step 4. Plan the Next Discovery Cycle

Based on what you learned, what do you know now that you did not know before? What does the user need to test next? Plan the next round of validation or market entry.

## Epistemic Grounding

Before designing customer interviews or segmentation, ground your understanding in what actually happens in the user's market.

Ask the user to describe:
1. A real customer conversation that surprised them (what did they believe before, what do they believe now)
2. A customer who said no (why did they pass, and what would have made them say yes)
3. A customer who said yes (why did they buy, what problem were they actually trying to solve)

Do not proceed without at least one of these stories. If the user has not talked to customers yet, start with outbound research and interviews before any other module.

## Examples

### Worked Example 1: Beachhead Too Broad (Discovery Failure)

**Stated customer:** "VP of Sales at mid-market software companies"

This is too broad. "Mid-market" ranges from $50M to $500M revenue. Sales cycles vary. The problems vary. Budget authority varies. This is a market segment, not a beachhead.

**What to do instead:** Ask the user to narrow it. "What size company? What is the ACV? What is your best customer so far?" Keep asking until you have something like: "A VP of Sales at a 15-100 person software company selling to construction companies, with $300K-$2M ACV."

Now you have a beachhead. You could name ten people in that segment. You know who they reference. You know their budget cycles.

### Worked Example 2: Assumption Testing (Discovery Success)

**Assumption:** "Construction company owners don't care about compliance. They only care about speed."

**Certainty:** Low (one customer mentioned speed, so the user believes this)

**Impact:** High (if true, the positioning changes. If false, the whole go-to-market is wrong)

**Experiment:** Conduct five interviews with construction company owners. Do not lead with speed. Ask an open-ended question about their biggest problem with their current vendor. Listen for whether compliance, risk, or speed comes up first.

**Result:** Four of five mentioned compliance and audit risk first. Speed came up second. The assumption was wrong.

**Output:** Mark the assumption as "REFUTED. Update positioning to lead with compliance."

## Troubleshooting

| Problem | Cause | Response |
|---------|-------|----------|
| "My beachhead segment is so specific nobody buys from it" | Segment is too narrow | Expand upward one level. A beachhead that is real but small beats a market that is large but imaginary. Go smaller, not larger. |
| "All my interviews confirm my hypothesis" | Selection bias or leading questions | You are interviewing people too similar to you or asking questions that have only one answer. Widen the interview pool. Use the Mom Test approach: ask about their current situation, not your solution. |
| "Everyone tells me they would buy it, but no one actually does" | Politeness bias in interviews | This is the most common discovery trap. Ask "Would you buy at $X by [date]?" not "Do you like this idea?" Watch for a no that isn't followed by an objection, that is usually politeness. The hard objection is the real signal. |
| "I do not have access to customers yet" | Timing issue | Do not start with interviews. Start with competitive research, public documents, and problem exploration. Build a target list. Ask for warm introductions. Do not skip discovery because access is hard, skip it because you do not believe it matters. |

## Best Practices

- **Do discovery in public.** Publish your assumptions and findings as you go. People will correct you. Correction is free education.
- **Interviewees should not know what you are building.** They should only know you are trying to understand their problem. The Mom Test is the canonical work here.
- **Competitor research is not about your keywords.** It is about the alternative the customer is actually using. That includes spreadsheets, contractors, consultants, and doing nothing.
- **Mark your confidence level on every claim.** Every statement about the customer should have a confidence tag: High / Medium / Low / Untested. When confidence is low, the next module tests it.
- **A beachhead with revenue beats a beachhead that is theoretically larger.** You are looking for the smallest customer segment you can dominate first, not the largest market you might eventually reach.
